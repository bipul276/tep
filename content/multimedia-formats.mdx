# Multimedia Systems: Audio Compression (MP3 vs MIDI), Video Standards (MPEG), and Animation Principles

## 1. Digital Audio Representation & Compression

Sound is an analog continuous mechanical wave caused by atmospheric pressure vibrations. To digitize acoustic signals for computer storage, systems employ **Pulse Code Modulation (PCM)**.

### 1.1 The Nyquist-Shannon Sampling Theorem

To perfectly reconstruct a band-limited continuous signal without destructive frequency overlapping (**aliasing**), the sampling frequency $f_s$ must be strictly greater than twice the highest frequency component $f_{\max}$ present in the audio spectrum:

$$f_s \ge 2 f_{\max}$$

Where:
- $f_{\text{Nyquist}} = 2 f_{\max}$ is the **Nyquist Rate**.
- If $f_s < 2 f_{\max}$, high-frequency tones fold down into the audible baseband as false frequency artifacts (**aliasing distortion**).
- For CD-quality audio (human hearing range spans $20\,\text{Hz} - 20\,\text{kHz}$):
$$f_{\max} = 20\,\text{kHz} \implies f_s = 2 \times 20\,\text{kHz} = 40\,\text{kHz}$$
  *(The standard CD sampling rate was engineered at $44.1\,\text{kHz}$ to accommodate practical analog anti-aliasing low-pass roll-off filters).*

### 1.2 Uncompressed Audio Bitrate Formula

$$\text{Bitrate (bps)} = f_s \times B \times N_{\text{channels}}$$

Where $f_s$ is sampling frequency (Hz), $B$ is bit depth (quantization bits per sample), and $N_{\text{channels}}$ is the number of audio channels ($1$ for Mono, $2$ for Stereo).

## 2. Audio Compression: MP3 vs. MIDI

### 2.1 The MP3 (MPEG-1 Audio Layer III) Psychoacoustic Model

MP3 is a lossy audio coding standard that achieves a dramatic $10:1$ to $12:1$ compression ratio by exploiting the physiological and neurological limitations of human auditory perception (**Psychoacoustics**):

```
 Acoustic Input ──► Filterbank (32 Subbands) ──► MDCT Transform ──► Bit Allocation ──► Huffman Coder
                          │                                                ▲
                          ▼                                                │
             [ FFT Spectrum Analysis ] ──► [ Psychoacoustic Model ] ───────┘
                                          (Masking Thresholds, SMR)
```

1. **Absolute Threshold of Hearing (ATH)**:
   - The human ear is not uniformly sensitive across frequencies. It exhibits peak sensitivity between $2\,\text{kHz} - 5\,\text{kHz}$ (the human speech range) and is drastically less sensitive at extreme frequencies ($< 50\,\text{Hz}$ and $> 16\,\text{kHz}$). Sounds below the quiet threshold curve are completely discarded.
2. **Frequency (Simultaneous) Masking**:
   - When a loud acoustic tone (the **masker**) is sounded at frequency $f_m$, it raises the threshold of audibility for adjacent, softer frequencies within that **Critical Band** (measured on the **Bark Scale**). The softer neighboring sounds become imperceptible to the human brain and are allocated zero bits.
3. **Temporal Masking**:
   - **Pre-masking (Backward)**: Occurs $5 - 20\,\text{ms}$ *before* an unexpected loud transient burst (e.g., a cymbal crash). The auditory cortex cannot register soft sounds immediately preceding the burst.
   - **Post-masking (Forward)**: Occurs for $50 - 200\,\text{ms}$ *after* a loud sound stops, as the mechanical basilar membrane gradually settles.
4. **Modified Discrete Cosine Transform (MDCT)**:
   - Converts time-domain subband samples into high-resolution frequency domain coefficients with $50\%$ overlapping windows, eliminating boundary blocking artifacts.

### 2.2 MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface)

**MIDI** is **NOT** digitized sound. It is a standardized communications protocol and hardware specification (established in 1983) that transmits **symbolic performance instructions** (event messages) between electronic musical instruments, synthesizers, and computers.

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Digital Audio (WAV / MP3)        VS         MIDI Protocol (MID)                              |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Stores actual sampled pressure              Stores musical instructions (Sheet music data):   |
|  waveform values (Gigabytes/Megabytes)       "Play Middle C, on Channel 1, with Velocity 95"  |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

#### MIDI Message Architecture
A MIDI packet consists of 8-bit bytes transmitted serially at $31.25\,\text{kbps}$. Every MIDI message begins with a **Status Byte** followed by one or two **Data Bytes**:

```
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| Status Byte (MSB = 1):  0x90 (Note On, Channel 1)                                     |
| Data Byte 1 (MSB = 0):  0x3C (Pitch = Note 60 = Middle C)                             |
| Data Byte 2 (MSB = 0):  0x64 (Velocity = 100 = Key strike force/volume)              |
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### 2.3 Comprehensive Comparison: MP3 vs. MIDI

```
+----------------------+------------------------------------------+------------------------------------------+
| Feature              | MP3 (Sampled Waveform Audio)             | MIDI (Synthesizer Event Protocol)        |
+----------------------+------------------------------------------+------------------------------------------+
| Content Nature       | Acoustic recording of physical sound     | Symbolic musical score / performance data|
| File Size            | Large ($\approx 1\,\text{MB/minute}$)     | Extremely Small ($\approx 10-30\,\text{KB/song}$) |
| Human Voice / Speech | Fully supported (Spoken voice, vocals)   | **Unsupported** (Cannot synthesize voice)|
| Sound Fidelity       | Identical playback across all devices    | Dependent on target sound card/synth chip|
| Editability          | Difficult (Spectral audio DSP editing)   | Trivial (Change tempo, pitch, instrument)|
| Bandwidth Need       | High (128 kbps to 320 kbps)              | Negligible ($< 1\,\text{kbps}$)          |
+----------------------+------------------------------------------+------------------------------------------+
```

## 3. Video Compression Standards (The MPEG Family)

Video is a rapid sequential presentation of static photographic frames (typically $24, 25, 30,$ or $60\,\text{fps}$). Uncompressed digital video demands astronomical storage and bandwidth. Video compression exploits two primary forms of redundancy:
1. **Spatial Redundancy (Intra-frame)**: Pixels within the same photographic frame exhibit high correlation (e.g., a blue sky). Reduced using Color Subsampling (YCbCr) and the **Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT)**.
2. **Temporal Redundancy (Inter-frame)**: Consecutive frames in a video sequence are nearly identical except for small object motions. Reduced using **Motion Estimation & Motion Compensation**.

---

### 3.1 The MPEG Frame Hierarchy: I, P, and B Frames

MPEG structures compressed video into an ordered sequence of three distinct frame types:

```
           Forward Motion Vector                  Forward & Backward Motion Vectors
           ┌──────────────────────┐               ┌────────────────────────────────┐
           │                      ▼               ▼                                │
      [ I-FRAME ]            [ P-FRAME ]    [ B-FRAME ]                      [ P-FRAME ]
      (Intra-Coded)          (Predicted)    (Bi-Directional)                 (Predicted)
```

```
+---------------------+-------------------+-----------------------------------+-----------------------------------+
| Frame Type          | Coding Mode       | Reference Requirements            | Compression & Relative Size       |
+---------------------+-------------------+-----------------------------------+-----------------------------------+
| **I-Frame**         | **Intra-coded**   | **None** (Self-contained static   | **Lowest compression**            |
| (Intra)             |                   | JPEG-like image; uses only DCT)   | (Largest frame size; $100\%$)     |
+---------------------+-------------------+-----------------------------------+-----------------------------------+
| **P-Frame**         | **Predictive**    | Preceding **I-frame or P-frame**  | **Moderate compression**          |
| (Predicted)         |                   | (Forward motion vectors)          | ($\approx 30 - 50\%$ of I-frame)  |
+---------------------+-------------------+-----------------------------------+-----------------------------------+
| **B-Frame**         | **Bi-directional**| Both **past AND future** frames   | **Highest compression**           |
| (Interpolated)      |                   | (Interpolates bidirectional MV)   | ($\approx 10 - 20\%$ of I-frame)  |
+---------------------+-------------------+-----------------------------------+-----------------------------------+
```

### 3.2 Group of Pictures (GOP) & Reordering Buffer

A **Group of Pictures (GOP)** begins with an **I-Frame** and establishes the periodic repetition pattern. 
- Example GOP: `I B B P B B P B B I`

#### Display Order vs. Decoding Order
Because a **B-Frame** depends mathematically on a future **P-Frame**, the video decoder **cannot** decode the B-frame until it has already received and decoded the future reference frame!

```
 Display Order (Viewer sees):     I_1    B_2    B_3    P_4    B_5    B_6    P_7
                                  │      ▲      ▲      │
                                  │      └──────┴──────┘ (B2 & B3 require P4!)
                                  ▼
 Transmission/Decoding Order:     I_1    P_4    B_2    B_3    P_7    B_5    B_6
```

> **Exam Key Fact**: In transmission and decoding order, future reference frames ($P_4$) must always precede the bidirectional frames ($B_2, B_3$) that reference them. The display device uses a **frame reordering buffer** to restore proper viewing cadence.

## 4. Principles of Computer Animation

Animation is the optical illusion of continuous motion created by the rapid presentation of sequential static images, relying on the physiological phenomenon of **Persistence of Vision** (where the human eye retains an image for approximately $1/16^{\text{th}}$ to $1/10^{\text{th}}$ of a second).

### 4.1 Keyframing vs. Tweening

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  [ Keyframe 1 ]  ══════════════►  [ Inbetween Frames ]  ══════════════►  [ Keyframe 2 ]       |
|  Ball at top of apex              Computer generates 22                  Ball squashes on     |
|  (Drawn by Lead Artist)           interpolated frames (Tweening)         impact with ground   |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

- **Keyframe**: A master frame drawn or configured by the animator that defines the extreme boundaries, pivotal poses, and timing landmarks of an action.
- **Tweening (Inbetweening)**: The process of generating intermediate frames between two keyframes to ensure smooth, continuous motion. In digital systems, tweening is automated via algorithmic interpolation:
  - **Linear Interpolation (Lerp)**: Constant velocity; produces robotic, unnatural motion.
  - **Bézier Curves & Ease-In / Ease-Out**: Modulates velocity using cubic polynomial curves to simulate physical inertia and gravity.

---

### 4.3 Morphing and Kinematics

- **Morphing**: Seamlessly transforming one visual image or 3D mesh into another through a two-step mathematical combination:
  1. **Warping**: Geometric coordinate distortion aligning key structural features (e.g., eyes to eyes, chin to chin).
  2. **Cross-Dissolving (Fade)**: Color interpolation between pixel values as geometry shifts.
- **Forward Kinematics (FK)**: Animating a joint hierarchy by specifying the rotational angles of parent bones down to child bones (e.g., Shoulder angle $\to$ Elbow angle $\to$ Wrist position).
- **Inverse Kinematics (IK)**: Specifying the target spatial coordinates of the end effector (e.g., the hand touches a doorknob), while an algorithmic solver computes the required angles for the shoulder and elbow automatically.

## 5. Worked Numerical Problems & Calculations

### Numerical 1: Raw Uncompressed CD Audio Storage
**Problem**: Calculate the uncompressed bitrate (in $\text{kbps}$) and the total storage space (in megabytes, where $1\,\text{MB} = 10^6\,\text{bytes}$) required to record a $74\text{-minute}$ stereo concert at standard CD quality ($f_s = 44.1\,\text{kHz}$, $16\text{-bit}$ resolution).

#### Solution:
1. **Bitrate Calculation**:
$$\text{Bitrate} = f_s \times B \times N_{\text{channels}} = 44,100\,\text{samples/sec} \times 16\,\text{bits/sample} \times 2\,\text{channels}$$
$$\text{Bitrate} = 1,411,200\,\text{bps} = 1,411.2\,\text{kbps}$$

2. **Total Seconds**:
$$T = 74\,\text{minutes} \times 60\,\text{sec/min} = 4,440\,\text{seconds}$$

3. **Total Data in Bytes**:
$$\text{Bytes/sec} = \frac{1,411,200\,\text{bits/sec}}{8} = 176,400\,\text{bytes/sec}$$
$$\text{Total Storage} = 176,400\,\text{bytes/sec} \times 4,440\,\text{sec} = 783,216,000\,\text{bytes} \approx 783.2\,\text{MB}$$

---

### Numerical 2: Raw 1080p Video Bitrate vs. MPEG Compression
**Problem**: A Full HD digital camera captures uncompressed video at $1920 \times 1080$ resolution, $24\text{-bit}$ TrueColor depth (RGB), and $30\,\text{frames per second}$. Calculate:
1. The uncompressed raw video data rate in $\text{Gbps}$.
2. The compression ratio required to stream this video over a standard $15\,\text{Mbps}$ broadband connection.

#### Solution:
1. **Uncompressed Frame Size**:
$$\text{Bits per Frame} = 1920 \times 1080 \times 24\,\text{bits} = 49,766,400\,\text{bits/frame}$$

2. **Raw Bitrate**:
$$\text{Bitrate} = 49,766,400\,\text{bits/frame} \times 30\,\text{frames/sec} = 1,492,992,000\,\text{bps} \approx 1.493\,\text{Gbps}$$

3. **Required Compression Ratio**:
$$\text{Ratio} = \frac{\text{Raw Bitrate}}{\text{Target Bitrate}} = \frac{1,492.99\,\text{Mbps}}{15\,\text{Mbps}} \approx 99.53 : 1 \approx 100 : 1$$

## 7. PCM Telephony Standard Bitrate & Nyquist Sampling

Pulse Code Modulation (PCM) is the digital standard for telephone voice transmission.

### 7.1 The 64 kbps Standard (DS0 Channel)
Human speech in telephony is filtered to a bandwidth of $4\text{ kHz}$ ($0\text{ to } 4000\text{ Hz}$).

By the **Nyquist-Shannon Sampling Theorem**:
$$f_s \ge 2 \cdot f_{\max} = 2 \times 4000\text{ Hz} = 8000\text{ samples/second}$$

In standard telephony PCM:
- **Sampling Frequency ($f_s$)**: $8000\text{ samples/s}$ ($1\text{ sample every } 125\;\mu\text{s}$).
- **Quantization Resolution ($n$)**: $8\text{ bits per sample}$ (using logarithmic companding: $\mu$-law in North America/Japan, A-law in Europe/India).

$$\text{Standard Voice Bitrate} = f_s \times n = 8000\text{ samples/s} \times 8\text{ bits/sample} = 64\text{ kbps}$$

This $64\text{ kbps}$ digital voice stream forms the fundamental **DS0 (Digital Signal 0)** channel in telecommunications.

### 7.2 Audio & Video Jitter in Real-Time Streaming
- **Jitter** is the statistical variance in packet arrival delay across a network.
- Caused by network congestion, queueing delays, and multi-path routing.
- Real-time protocols (RTP/RTSP) mitigate jitter using **playback buffers** (holding received packets temporarily to play them at a constant, uniform rate).

| Topic Segment | Critical Formulation / Invariant | Common Exam Trap |
|---|---|---|
| **Nyquist Theorem** | $f_s \ge 2 f_{\max}$; CD Audio uses **$44.1\,\text{kHz}$** | Thinking Nyquist rate is $f_s = f_{\max}$ (must be $\ge 2 f_{\max}$) |
| **MP3 Masking** | Simultaneous (Frequency) + Temporal (Pre/Post) | Believing MP3 is lossless (it is lossy psychoacoustic) |
| **MIDI Architecture** | Status Byte (MSB=1), Data Bytes (MSB=0); No speech | Thinking MIDI stores recorded waveforms like MP3 |
| **MPEG Frame Roles** | I = Intra (Anchor); P = Predicted; B = Bi-directional | Stating B-frames have the largest size (I-frames are largest) |
| **GOP Reordering** | Decode order puts future P-frame **before** B-frames | Assuming decoding order is identical to display order |
| **Squash & Stretch** | Invariant: $\text{Volume} = \text{Constant}$ | Forgetting volume preservation during visual deformation |
| **FK vs IK** | FK: Joint angles $\to$ Hand pos; IK: Hand pos $\to$ Joint angles | Inverting Kinematics directionality |
