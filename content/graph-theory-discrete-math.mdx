# Graph Theory: Handshaking Lemma, Euler/Hamilton Circuits, Planarity & Coloring

## 1. Formal Foundations & Graph Taxonomy

A **graph** $G = (V, E)$ consists of a non-empty set of **vertices (nodes)** $V(G)$ and a set of **edges (links)** $E(G)$, where each edge $e \in E$ connects a pair of vertices $\{u, v\} \subseteq V$.

### 1.1 Structural Types of Graphs
1. **Simple Graph**: Contains no self-loops (an edge connecting a vertex to itself) and no parallel edges (multiple edges connecting the exact same pair of vertices).
2. **Multigraph**: May contain parallel edges connecting the same pair of vertices, but no self-loops.
3. **Pseudograph**: May contain both parallel edges and self-loops.
4. **Directed Graph (Digraph)**: Each edge is an ordered pair $(u, v)$ with direction from source $u$ to target $v$.
5. **Undirected Graph**: Each edge is an unordered pair $\{u, v\}$.

---

## 2. Vertex Degrees & The Handshaking Lemma

In an undirected graph, the **degree (or valency)** of a vertex $v$, denoted $\deg(v)$, is the number of edges incident to $v$. A self-loop contributes **2** to the degree of its vertex.

### 2.1 The Handshaking Lemma (First Theorem of Graph Theory)

> **Theorem (Leonhard Euler, 1736)**:
> In any undirected graph $G = (V, E)$:
> $$\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = 2|E|$$

#### Formal Mathematical Proof:
Every edge $e = \{u, v\} \in E$ connects two endpoints $u$ and $v$. When we sum the degrees of all vertices $\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v)$, each edge is counted exactly once at each of its two terminal vertices. Consequently, every edge contributes exactly **2** to the total sum of degrees. Therefore, the sum of all vertex degrees must equal twice the number of edges: $2|E|$. $\blacksquare$

---

### 2.2 Invariants and Corollaries of the Handshaking Lemma

#### Corollary 1: Odd Degree Vertices are Always Even in Number
> In every undirected graph, the number of vertices having **odd degree** is **always even**.

**Proof**:
Partition the vertex set $V$ into two disjoint subsets: $V_{\text{even}}$ (vertices with even degree) and $V_{\text{odd}}$ (vertices with odd degree).
$$\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = \sum_{v \in V_{\text{even}}} \deg(v) + \sum_{v \in V_{\text{odd}}} \deg(v) = 2|E|$$
- The total sum $2|E|$ is an **even integer**.
- $\sum_{v \in V_{\text{even}}} \deg(v)$ is a sum of even numbers, which is **even**.
- Therefore:
$$\sum_{v \in V_{\text{odd}}} \deg(v) = 2|E| - \sum_{v \in V_{\text{even}}} \deg(v) = \text{Even} - \text{Even} = \textbf{Even}$$
- A sum of odd integers can be even **if and only if** the number of terms in the sum is **even**.
- Hence, $|V_{\text{odd}}|$ must be an even integer. $\blacksquare$

#### Corollary 2: $k$-Regular Graphs
A graph is **$k$-regular** if every vertex has degree $k$ ($\deg(v) = k \quad \forall v \in V$).
$$n \cdot k = 2|E| \implies |E| = \frac{n \cdot k}{2}$$
*(If $k$ is odd, the number of vertices $n$ must be even for such a graph to exist).*

### 2.3 Graphic Sequences: The Havel-Hakimi Theorem

A sequence of non-negative integers $d_1 \ge d_2 \ge \dots \ge d_n$ is called **graphic** if there exists a simple graph whose degree sequence is precisely $(d_1, d_2, \dots, d_n)$.

```
Algorithm Havel_Hakimi:
  Input: Degree sequence S = (d₁, d₂, ..., dₙ) in descending order.
  Repeat:
    1. If any dᵢ < 0: Return FALSE.
    2. If all dᵢ == 0: Return TRUE.
    3. Remove the first element d₁.
    4. Subtract 1 from the next d₁ elements in the list.
    5. Re-sort the remaining sequence in descending order.
```

#### Worked Example:
Determine if $S = (5, 4, 3, 3, 2, 2, 1)$ is graphic:
1. $d_1 = 5$. Subtract 1 from next 5 elements: $(3, 2, 2, 1, 1, 1)$.
2. Next $d_1 = 3$. Subtract 1 from next 3 elements: $(1, 1, 0, 1, 1)$. Re-sort $\implies (1, 1, 1, 1, 0)$.
3. Next $d_1 = 1$. Subtract 1 from next 1 element: $(0, 1, 1, 0)$. Re-sort $\implies (1, 1, 0, 0)$.
4. Next $d_1 = 1$. Subtract 1 from next 1 element: $(0, 0, 0)$.
5. All zeros reached! Therefore, $S$ is a **valid graphic sequence**.

## 3. Special Families of Graphs

| Graph Family | Notation | Vertices ($|V|$) | Edges ($|E|$) | Degree of Vertices |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| **Complete Graph** | $K_n$ | $n$ | $\binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ | $(n-1)$-regular |
| **Cycle Graph** | $C_n$ ($n \ge 3$) | $n$ | $n$ | 2-regular |
| **Path Graph** | $P_n$ ($n$ vertices) | $n$ | $n - 1$ | Endpoints: 1, Internal: 2 |
| **Wheel Graph** | $W_n$ ($C_{n-1} + \text{Hub}$) | $n$ | $2(n - 1)$ | Hub: $n-1$, Rim: 3 |
| **Complete Bipartite** | $K_{m,n}$ | $m + n$ | $m \cdot n$ | $m$ vertices of degree $n$, $n$ vertices of degree $m$ |
| **$k$-dimensional Hypercube** | $Q_k$ | $2^k$ | $k \cdot 2^{k-1}$ | $k$-regular |

### 3.1 Bipartite Graphs & Characterization Theorem

A graph $G = (V, E)$ is **bipartite** if its vertex set $V$ can be partitioned into two disjoint subsets $V_1$ and $V_2$ ($V = V_1 \cup V_2, V_1 \cap V_2 = \emptyset$) such that every edge in $E$ connects a vertex in $V_1$ to a vertex in $V_2$ (no edge connects two vertices in the same partition).

> **König's Theorem (1936)**:
> A graph $G$ is **bipartite** if and only if it contains **NO odd-length cycles**.

**Maximum Edges in a Simple Bipartite Graph with $n$ Vertices**:
$$\text{Max Edges} = \left\lfloor \frac{n}{2} \right\rfloor \times \left\lceil \frac{n}{2} \right\rceil = \left\lfloor \frac{n^2}{4} \right\rfloor$$

## 4. Walks, Trails, Paths, Circuits, and Cycles

To avoid standard competitive exam terminology confusion:

| Term | Edge Repetition Allowed? | Vertex Repetition Allowed? | Open or Closed? |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| **Walk** | **Yes** | **Yes** | Either |
| **Trail** | **No** (All edges distinct) | **Yes** | Either |
| **Path (Simple Path)** | **No** | **No** (All vertices distinct) | Open ($u \neq v$) |
| **Circuit** | **No** (All edges distinct) | **Yes** | Closed (Starts & ends at same vertex) |
| **Cycle (Simple Cycle)** | **No** | **No** (Except start = end) | Closed |

## 5. Eulerian Graphs vs. Hamiltonian Graphs

```text
Eulerian: Focuses on EDGES (Traverse EVERY edge exactly once)
Hamiltonian: Focuses on VERTICES (Visit EVERY vertex exactly once)
```

### 5.1 Eulerian Circuits and Trails

1. **Eulerian Trail**: A walk in a graph that visits **every edge exactly once**.
2. **Eulerian Circuit (Eulerian Tour)**: A closed walk that visits **every edge exactly once** and returns to the starting vertex.
3. **Eulerian Graph**: A graph that contains an Eulerian circuit.

#### Necessary and Sufficient Conditions:
- **Eulerian Circuit Condition**: A connected undirected graph has an Eulerian circuit $\iff$ **every vertex has an EVEN degree**.
- **Eulerian Trail Condition**: A connected undirected graph has an Eulerian trail (but not a circuit) $\iff$ **EXACTLY TWO vertices have an ODD degree**.
  - The trail must start at one of the odd-degree vertices and terminate at the other odd-degree vertex!
- **Directed Graph Condition**: A weakly connected directed graph has an Eulerian circuit $\iff$ for every vertex $v$, $\text{in-degree}(v) = \text{out-degree}(v)$.

---

### 5.2 Hamiltonian Cycles and Paths

1. **Hamiltonian Path**: A simple path that visits **every vertex in the graph exactly once**.
2. **Hamiltonian Cycle**: A closed cycle that visits **every vertex exactly once** (except the start/end vertex).
3. **Hamiltonian Graph**: A graph that contains a Hamiltonian cycle.

*(Note: Finding whether a graph is Hamiltonian is **NP-Complete**, whereas checking if a graph is Eulerian is solvable in linear time $O(|V| + |E|)$).*

#### Sufficient Conditions for Hamiltonian Graphs:
- **Dirac's Theorem (1952)**: If $G$ is a simple graph with $n \ge 3$ vertices such that:
$$\deg(v) \ge \frac{n}{2} \quad \forall v \in V$$
  then $G$ is Hamiltonian.
- **Ore's Theorem (1960)**: If $G$ is a simple graph with $n \ge 3$ vertices such that for every pair of non-adjacent vertices $u$ and $v$:
$$\deg(u) + \deg(v) \ge n$$
  then $G$ is Hamiltonian.

## 6. Planar Graphs & Kuratowski's Theorem

A graph is **planar** if it can be drawn in the 2D plane in such a way that no two edges cross or intersect each other except at their common endpoints.

### 6.1 Euler's Planar Graph Formula

> **Theorem**: For any connected planar graph drawn in a plane with $V$ vertices, $E$ edges, and $F$ regions (faces, including the unbounded exterior region):
> $$V - E + F = 2$$

For a disconnected planar graph with $k$ connected components:
$$V - E + F = 1 + k$$

---

### 6.2 Planar Graph Invariant Inequalities

In any planar graph, every region is bounded by at least 3 edges ($2E \ge 3F$):

$$E \le 3V - 6 \quad (\text{for } V \ge 3)$$

If the planar graph contains **no cycles of length 3** (triangle-free, such as bipartite planar graphs), then every region is bounded by at least 4 edges ($2E \ge 4F$):

$$E \le 2V - 4 \quad (\text{for } V \ge 3)$$

#### Mathematical Proof that $K_5$ is Non-Planar:
- For $K_5$: $V = 5$, $E = \binom{5}{2} = 10$.
- Test inequality: $E \le 3V - 6 \implies 10 \le 3(5) - 6 = 15 - 6 = 9$.
- $10 \le 9$ is **FALSE!** Therefore, $K_5$ cannot be planar.

#### Mathematical Proof that $K_{3,3}$ is Non-Planar:
- $K_{3,3}$ is bipartite, so it contains no odd cycles (triangle-free).
- $V = 6$, $E = 3 \times 3 = 9$.
- Test triangle-free inequality: $E \le 2V - 4 \implies 9 \le 2(6) - 4 = 12 - 4 = 8$.
- $9 \le 8$ is **FALSE!** Therefore, $K_{3,3}$ cannot be planar.

---

### 6.3 Kuratowski's Theorem (Planarity Characterization)

> **Theorem (Kazimierz Kuratowski, 1930)**:
> A finite graph is planar **if and only if** it does not contain a subgraph that is homeomorphic to (or can be formed by edge subdivision of) $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$.

```text
       K₅ (Complete on 5)                     K₃,₃ (Complete Bipartite 3x3)
              (v1)                                  (A1)    (A2)    (A3)
             / \ \ \                                  \ \  / / \  / /
           (v2)-(v3) \ \                                \  \/   \/ /
            | X  |    \ \                                \ /\   /\ /
           (v4)-(v5)---v1                               (B1)    (B2)    (B3)
```

## 7. Graph Coloring & Chromatic Number $\chi(G)$

A **proper vertex coloring** of a graph is an assignment of colors to vertices such that no two adjacent vertices share the same color.
- **Chromatic Number $\chi(G)$**: The minimum number of colors needed to properly color $G$.

### 7.1 Chromatic Numbers of Standard Graphs

| Graph Family | Chromatic Number $\chi(G)$ | Explanation |
|---|:---:|---|
| **Complete Graph $K_n$** | $n$ | Every vertex is adjacent to every other vertex |
| **Null / Empty Graph $N_n$** | $1$ | No edges exist |
| **Bipartite Graph $K_{m,n}$** | $2$ (for $E \ge 1$) | Color partition $V_1$ with Color 1, $V_2$ with Color 2 |
| **Tree $T$** | $2$ (for $n \ge 2$) | Every tree is bipartite |
| **Cycle Graph $C_n$ ($n$ even)** | $2$ | Alternating colors |
| **Cycle Graph $C_n$ ($n$ odd)** | $3$ | Odd cycle requires a 3rd color to close |
| **Wheel Graph $W_n$** | $3$ (if $n$ is odd) / $4$ (if $n$ is even) | Rim takes 2 or 3 colors; Central hub takes 1 extra color |

### 7.2 Theorems on Chromatic Numbers
1. **Four Color Theorem (Appel & Haken, 1976)**: Every planar graph has $\chi(G) \le 4$.
2. **Brook's Theorem**: For any connected graph $G$:
$$\chi(G) \le \Delta(G)$$
   unless $G$ is a complete graph $K_n$ or an odd cycle $C_{2k+1}$, in which case $\chi(G) = \Delta(G) + 1$ (where $\Delta(G)$ is the maximum vertex degree).

## 8. UGC NET & Bihar STET PYQ Practice Patterns

### Question 1 (UGC NET Dec 2019 — Handshaking Lemma)
A graph $G$ has 21 edges, 3 vertices of degree 4, and other vertices of degree 3. How many total vertices does $G$ have?

**Solution**:
1. By Handshaking Lemma: $\sum \deg(v) = 2|E| = 2 \times 21 = 42$.
2. Let $k$ be the number of vertices of degree 3.
   Total vertices $V = 3 + k$.
3. Sum of degrees:
$$
\begin{aligned}
   (3 \times 4) + (k \times 3) &= 42 \\
   12 + 3k &= 42 \\
   3k &= 30 \implies k = 10
   \end{aligned}
$$
4. Total vertices $V = 3 + 10 = \mathbf{13}$.
**Answer**: 13 vertices.

### Question 2 (UGC NET June 2020 — Planar Graph Euler Formula)
A connected planar graph has 30 edges and divides the plane into 20 regions. How many vertices does the graph have?

**Solution**:
1. Euler's planar formula: $V - E + F = 2$.
2. Given $E = 30$, $F = 20$.
$$V - 30 + 20 = 2 \implies V - 10 = 2 \implies V = \mathbf{12}$$
**Answer**: 12 vertices.

### Question 3 (UGC NET 2021 — Eulerian Graph Criteria)
For which values of $n$ does the complete graph $K_n$ contain an Eulerian circuit?

**Solution**:
- In $K_n$, every vertex has degree $n - 1$.
- An Eulerian circuit exists $\iff$ every vertex has an **even degree**.
- Therefore, $n - 1$ must be even $\implies n$ must be **odd**.
**Answer**: For all **odd values of $n$** ($n = 3, 5, 7, \dots$).
